# Stepper Motor Driver

  I built a stepper motor driver on a breadboard using discrete components and an Arduino to control it all. This process taught me much about transistors and their limits, high frequency electronics and programming for efficiency. 

  This project starts off with an H bridge. Each H-bridge can drive one inductive load, or one coil of a stepper motor. Thus I need two as my stepper motor is two phase. Pictured below is the schematic of the dual H-bridge I built on the breadboard. An H-bridge works by having only one transistor on each leg of the bridge open at a time (or none at all). For example, for current to flow from left to right across the winding, I would open the high side transistor on the left leg, and the low side transistor on the right leg. This allows me to control the direction of the current across the coil. You can control the rate of the dissipation of the current in the winding by having either both high sides/low sides open, which allows current to dissipate slowly, or by opening the winding to the opposite current flow, which causes the current to dissipate rapidly. I hope to implement a dynamic current decay model in the future, where I can switch between fast and slow decay based on the situation, but right now, I just use the slow decay. In the case where all the transistors are off, the current in the winding has no where to go, causing voltage to build up and possibly kill the transistors. Thus you can see I put flyback diodes on each transistor so that the current has a safe path to go through the power supply, rather than the transistors. At the bottom of the half bridge I have a master chopper transistor that I can use to control the exact amount of current the coil gets. This will be useful later. 

  For each of the high side transistors, I have another transistor attached to the gate. This because when I ordered the MOSFETS off Amazon, the PMOS transistors I got were not working, so I had to improvise and use NMOS as high side, which requires some extra circuitry to use. For an NMOS to be fully open, the gate voltage has to be several volts higher than the source voltage (V_gs > 2.0). This leads to a problem, if my system is driven off of 10V, how do I supply 12 volts or greater to the gate of the high side drivers? As you can see below in the full schematic, I used a Dickson charge pump driven by an LM555 timer set up in a astable mode at roughly 700 Hz. I used this method of boosting voltage because it used parts that I had lying around. This method has its own problems, i.e. the voltage drops a significant amount with the MOSFETS switching fast. This could be solved in the future by either just buying PMOS or using a DC-DC boost converter. I take this boosted voltage and put it through a resistor that has an NMOS on the low side of it. And this I take the voltage across the bottom of the resistor and put it on the gate of the high side drivers. When this secondary NMOS is given voltage at its gate, the bottom of the resistor is shorted to ground, thus the high side gate voltage is 0 and the high side NMOS turns off. However when the secondary NMOS's gate is shorted to ground, the resistor no longer forms a complete circuit and thus acts like a wire, meaning the voltage at the bottom of the resistor is the boosted voltage from the Dickson charge pump, turning the high side NMOS on. Notice how this inverts the logic for the high side drivers. HIGH input on the secondary means that the high side is off, and LOW on the secondary means high side in on. 

  To test the motor and the driver, I wrote some simple code (full/half stepping), where I turn the coils on and off, while keeping the master choppers on, in a pattern called full stepping. Wave stepping is very similar however while full stepping has two coils active at a time, wave stepping only has one. They share the same resolution however since each time I switch the states of the windings, it moves the rotor by one step. For my motor its 1.8 degrees of rotation per step. This method provides the highest torque and speed since the coils are fully energized each step. However for more precise applications, 1.8 degrees of rotation per step is simply too much, and often this results in a not smooth feeling of the rotor. The next implementation that I tried was the half step. In this method, I alternate between having two coils energized and one coil being energized. To kind of conceptualize this, imagine the rotor pointing towards coil A. Then we can turn on coil B and the rotor will point in between. Then we can turn off coil A and the rotor will point to just coil B. And we can repeat this by turning on coil A in a inverted polarity and so on and so forth. As you can see this allows us to double our resolution with the rotor only turning 0.9 degrees per step. However this does come with its drawbacks. Alternating the number of coils on makes the torque uneven between steps, and the higher number of steps means that the fixed speed of the Arduino, the max speed is halved. 

  I wanted to push the idea of increasing the number of steps further, so I tried to implement micro stepping. The idea is that the amount of current in coil A follows a sine wave while the current in coil B follows the same wave shifted by 90deg or a cosine wave. This means that instead of the rotor snapping from coil A to coil B, it can follow a smoother path between them. Because MCU's don't have infinite resolution, we can instead follow a discretized version of the sine waves and we can vary the resolution we want, at the cost of torque(to a limit) and speed. Since I didn't have resistors big enough to handle the current of the motor, I had to estimate the current through each coil by measuring the resistance of each coil. With some logic, I can get the PWM values the Arduino need to output to the master choppers at the bottom of each H-bridge to get the max load of 1 amp through the coils (which is the current limit of the motors). Then we can simply scale this by the sine wave and sample it at evenly spaced intervals. Then its as simple as choosing the current direction based on sign of the sine wave at the current step, and modulating current using the master choppers. Some errors I ran into with this process is that there is a max limit to how fast I can PWM the system because MOSFETS have switching times, and some of my transistors get got because they don't have time to fully open. This can be remedied by adding built in wait times. The matter is adding the right delay so that it doesn't stall due to it rotating too slow, or stall because the rotor cant keep up with the changing field. Another problem was that I needed to optimize my code so that I could get a decent enough speed out of the motor. An inefficient stepping algorithm means that it takes milliseconds per step, leading to extremely low RPMs. This is why I decided to precompute the PWM values and try to minimize logic in the step function.
